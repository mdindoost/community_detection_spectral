\subsection{Community detection}\label{sec:background:cd}

Community detection partitions the vertices of a graph into groups that are
densely connected internally and sparsely connected to the
rest~\cite{fortunato2010community, fortunato202220}. The problem has no single
agreed formalization, and the choice of objective is itself a modelling decision.

The most widely used objective is modularity~\cite{newman2004finding,
newman2006modularity}. For a graph $G$ with $m$ edges and a partition $P$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modularity}
Q(P, G) \;=\; \sum_{C \in P} \left[ \frac{m_C}{m} - \gamma
\left( \frac{D_C}{2m} \right)^{\!2} \right],
\end{equation}
where $m_C$ is the number of edges inside community $C$, $D_C$ is the sum of the
degrees of its vertices, and $\gamma$ is a resolution parameter equal to one in
the standard definition. The first term rewards internal edges and the second
subtracts what a random graph with the same degree sequence would produce.
Maximizing $Q$ is NP-hard~\cite{brandes2007modularity}, so the objective is
approached heuristically, and it carries a resolution
limit~\cite{fortunato2007resolution}: at $\gamma = 1$ it cannot resolve
communities smaller than a scale set by $m$, regardless of how well separated
they are.

Algorithms divide by whether the number of communities is an input. Louvain
optimizes~\eqref{eq:modularity} by greedy local moves followed by
aggregation~\cite{blondel2008fast}, and Leiden adds a refinement phase that
guarantees well-connected communities~\cite{traag2019louvain}. Infomap instead
minimizes the description length of a random walk~\cite{rosvall2008maps}, and
label propagation iterates a local majority rule to a fixed
point~\cite{raghavan2007near}. None of these is told how many communities to
find. Multilevel graph partitioners are: Metis~\cite{karypis1998metis} and
Graclus~\cite{dhillon2007weighted} coarsen the graph, cut the coarsest level into
a requested $k$ parts, and refine while uncoarsening, with Metis enforcing a
balance constraint on part sizes and Graclus optimizing normalized cut or ratio
association without one. Flow-based clustering with a coarsening schedule, as in
MLR-MCL~\cite{satuluri2009scalable}, sits between the two, since its count
follows from an inflation parameter.

The consequence is that the number of communities an algorithm reports is a
property of the algorithm and its parameters as much as of the graph. Two
methods run on the same network routinely return counts that differ by orders of
magnitude.

\subsection{Judging a partition}\label{sec:background:eval}

Two kinds of criterion are in use, and they answer different questions.

Internal criteria score a partition against the graph itself. Modularity and
conductance are the common choices, and both are computed from the edges of the
graph being described. External criteria compare a partition against reference
communities obtained independently of the graph structure. Normalized mutual
information is the most frequently reported, and it has a known bias: its value
rises with the number of clusters, so a finer partition scores higher without
being better. Hamann et al.~\cite{hamann2016structure} identify this as making
NMI unsuitable for comparing partitions of sparsified networks, where many small
communities are expected, and recommend chance-corrected indices in its place.
The adjusted Rand index and adjusted mutual
information~\cite{vinh2010information} subtract the value a random partition of
the same shape would obtain, which is what "adjusted" means. Where reference
communities overlap and are numerous, average best-match $F_1$ over reference
communities is used instead, and it carries the same granularity bias as NMI
without a standard correction.

Whether reference communities are the right target at all is contested.
Peel et al.~\cite{peel2017ground} show that node metadata is not ground truth for
community structure and that treating it as such licenses invalid conclusions.
Hric et al.~\cite{hric2014community} find structural communities and
metadata groups to disagree systematically across many networks. Yang and
Leskovec~\cite{yang2012defining} define the functional reference communities
distributed with several widely used datasets, which are overlapping and small.

Because real networks supply no unambiguous answer, synthetic benchmarks with
planted communities are standard. The LFR benchmark~\cite{lancichinetti2008benchmark}
generates graphs with power-law degree and community-size distributions and a
mixing parameter controlling the fraction of inter-community edges. Stochastic
block models and their degree-corrected variant~\cite{karrer2011stochastic}
provide a generative alternative in which degree heterogeneity can be varied
independently of community structure.

\subsection{Graph sparsification}\label{sec:background:spars}

Sparsification replaces a graph with a subgraph on the same vertex set and fewer
edges, chosen so that some property of the original is preserved. Which property
is preserved, and how tightly, distinguishes the methods.

The strongest results are spectral. Bencz\'ur and
Karger~\cite{benczur2015randomized} preserve every cut to within $1 \pm \epsilon$
by sampling edges with probability proportional to connectivity and reweighting
the survivors. Spielman and Srivastava~\cite{spielman2008graph} preserve the
Laplacian quadratic form by sampling according to effective
resistance~\cite{lovasz1993random, doyle1984random}, giving a spectral sparsifier
with $O(n \log n / \epsilon^2)$ edges~\cite{spielman2014nearly}. These guarantees
concern the Laplacian spectrum. Community structure is related to that spectrum
through the eigenvectors that encode cuts~\cite{von2007tutorial,
shi2000normalized, newman2013spectral} without being determined by it.

The methods used in practice are cheaper and carry no such guarantee.
Similarity-based methods rank each edge by the overlap of its endpoints'
neighbourhoods and keep the highest, as in the local sparsification of Satuluri
et al.~\cite{satuluri2011local}. Degree-based methods score an edge from its
endpoint degrees alone; DSpar~\cite{liu2023dspar} approximates the effective
resistance of $(u,v)$ by $1/d_u + 1/d_v$, which is within a factor of the true
value that depends on the spectral gap. Backbone extraction keeps edges that are
improbable under a local null, as in the disparity
filter~\cite{serrano2009extracting} and its noise-corrected
successor~\cite{coscia2017backboning}. Hamann et al.~\cite{hamann2016structure}
introduced local filtering variants, including Local Degree, Local Similarity and
K-Neighbor, and released implementations now distributed in standard graph
libraries. Learned methods train a model to select the
subgraph~\cite{wu2020gsgan}.

Sparsification sits inside a wider family of graph reduction techniques that also
includes coarsening, which merges vertices into supernodes, and condensation,
which synthesizes a small graph. Hashemi et al.~\cite{hashemi2024survey} survey
the three together. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2024demystifying} benchmark twelve
sparsifiers against sixteen properties on fourteen networks and report that no
single method preserves all of them, that the choice should follow the downstream
task, that pruning fragments graphs, and that fidelity to the full graph's
partition degrades monotonically with the prune rate.

\subsection{Redundancy in real networks}\label{sec:background:redundancy}

That a graph can lose most of its edges and keep its structure is an empirical
regularity with a formal counterpart. The \emph{metric backbone} of a weighted
graph is the union of its all-pairs shortest paths, so an edge outside it is
redundant in the precise sense that no shortest path uses it. Correia et
al.~\cite{correia2023contact} find the backbone to be a small fraction of the
edges of real contact networks while community structure is retained, and
Dreveton et al.~\cite{dreveton2024metric} prove that deleting every edge outside
the backbone preserves community structure on a broad class of weighted random
graphs with communities.

A parallel line asks the same question of sampling rather than of pruning.
Leskovec and Faloutsos~\cite{leskovec2006sampling} set the graph-sampling
problem, Maiya and Berger-Wolf~\cite{maiya2010sampling} sample explicitly to
preserve community structure, and Blagus et
al.~\cite{blagus2015sampling} report that sampling can manufacture the appearance
of community structure rather than preserve it. How much redundancy a particular
graph carries, and how to measure it before deciding to sparsify, is not settled.

\subsection{Sparsifying before detecting communities}\label{sec:background:pipeline}

Satuluri et al.~\cite{satuluri2011local} proposed sparsifying a graph before
clustering it, reporting speedups commonly between $10\times$ and $50\times$ with
little or no loss of quality, and improved accuracy for two of the four
clustering algorithms tested, both of which take the number of communities as an
input.

The pipeline was adopted and extended. Reference implementations entered graph
libraries~\cite{hamann2016structure}, sparsification was applied before community
detection at industrial scale~\cite{satuluri2020simclusters}, generative models
were trained to produce the subgraph~\cite{wu2020gsgan}, and triangle-aware
spectral sparsifiers were introduced to explain why the approach
works~\cite{sotiropoulos2021triangle}. It remains active: Socievole and Pizzuti
weight a graph by effective resistance, apply a kernel, sparsify and then
detect~\cite{socievole2024community, socievole2026effective}, and further
effective-resistance and $k$-core variants have appeared
since~\cite{pari2026effective, setiadi2025community}. Peng et
al.~\cite{peng2014accelerating} reduce by $k$-core before detecting, which
removes vertices as well as edges.

One analysis identifies a regime in which the approach provably pays.
Laeuchli~\cite{laeuchli2020fast} studies community detection on sparsified dense
planted-partition graphs with spectral solvers, where detection cost is
superlinear in the number of edges and the planted structure survives heavy
deletion.

\subsection{Open methodological questions}\label{sec:background:method}

The literature has raised several questions about how this pipeline should be
measured, and has answered them inconsistently.

The first concerns which graph a quality score is computed on. Satuluri et
al.\ state in their \S4.3 that clusters obtained from a sparsified graph cannot
be scored on that same graph, because doing so says nothing about how well the
sparsification retained the structure of the original, and they report every
conductance on the original graph. They also fix the number of communities once
per network and supply it to every arm, and they charge sparsification time to
the reported speedup. Practice since has varied. In the released code of the
benchmark of Chen et al.~\cite{chen2024demystifying}, modularity for the
sparsified condition is computed on the sparsified graph. Two recent
sparsify-then-detect papers report objective values above what their graphs
admit: modularity $0.87$ on the American College Football
network~\cite{girvan2002community}, whose maximum over all partitions is
$0.6046$~\cite{pari2026effective}, and $0.63$ on Zachary's karate
club~\cite{zachary1977information}, whose maximum is
$0.4198$~\cite{setiadi2025community}.

The second concerns granularity. Sparsification fragments graphs, algorithms that
choose their own granularity return more communities on the result, and the
agreement measures in common use reward that. The bias is documented and the
chance-corrected alternatives exist, but comparisons at matched community counts
are not standard.

The third concerns cost. Sparsification is itself a computation.
Gottesb\"{u}ren et al.~\cite{gottesburen2025linear} observe that its overhead can
cancel the speedup it is intended to produce, and end-to-end timings including
that overhead are rarely reported.

The fourth concerns attribution. A method that selects edges using degrees may
produce its effect through the degree sequence rather than through community
structure. Separating the two calls for a null model that holds the degree
sequence fixed while destroying communities, for which the standard instruments
are the configuration model~\cite{molloy1995critical} and the degree-preserving
edge swap~\cite{maslov2002specificity}.

These four questions motivate the evaluation protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.
