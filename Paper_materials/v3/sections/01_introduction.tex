% ---- B1: the practical question ------------------------------------
Community detection on large graphs is bounded by edges, not by nodes. A social
network with a hundred million accounts may carry ten billion links, and every
algorithm that partitions it---modularity optimization, flow simulation, label
propagation, spectral bisection---pays for those links on every pass. The nodes
cannot be discarded, because a community assignment is required for each of
them; sampling vertices changes the problem rather than shrinking it. Edges are
different. If most of them are redundant for the purpose of recovering community
structure, then removing them before detection would buy speed for nothing. That
possibility has motivated fifteen years of work on sparsify-then-detect
pipelines, and the methods that implement it now ship in widely used graph
libraries and in at least one production system.

% ---- B2: the answer the field has, and how it was actually scoped ----
The founding answer is affirmative, and it is more careful than it is usually
remembered. Satuluri, Parthasarathy and Ruan~\cite{satuluri2011local} introduced
local similarity sparsification, in which every node retains the incident edges
whose endpoints share the most neighbours, and reported speedups ``often in the
range 10--50, with little or no deterioration in the quality of the resulting
clusters,'' adding that ``for at least two of the four clustering algorithms, our
sparsification consistently enables higher clustering accuracies.'' Every clause
of that sentence is qualified. The two algorithms are fixed-$k$,
balance-constrained cut partitioners; the accuracy is agreement with external
ground-truth communities, not any objective computed on the graph; the speedups
are measured against clustering runs that take hours; and the graphs on which the
gains are largest are dense---average degrees of 65, 76 and 94---and, in two
cases, known to contain a substantial fraction of spurious edges. The authors also
report, in a synthetic sweep, that their method ``actually outperforms the
original clustering starting from degree 50.'' That single sentence names a
boundary, and, as far as we can determine, no subsequent work returns to it.

% ---- B3: the scope drift, stated without accusation ------------------
What propagated was the headline rather than its scope. ``Sparsify before you
detect'' became a general recommendation: applied to modularity optimizers rather
than cut partitioners, to sparse graphs rather than dense ones, and to
graph-internal objectives rather than external references. None of these
extensions is unreasonable on its face, and each was made in good faith. But
together they moved the claim off the territory where it was established, and the
degree threshold that would have marked the move was not carried along.

% ---- B4: the lost control and what its absence costs (THE HINGE) -----
One methodological requirement was lost with it, and it is the reason the boundary
has stayed invisible. A sparsifier for community detection is built to remove
edges that lie between communities in preference to edges that lie inside them. A
graph so treated is therefore a graph on which any partition looks better: the
very edges that penalize a partition have been preferentially deleted. Measuring
the quality of a partition on the sparsified graph does not measure how well
sparsification preserved community structure; it measures how thoroughly the
sparsifier did its own job. The correct comparison scores the partition obtained
from the sparsified graph and the partition obtained from the original graph on
the \emph{same} graph---the original one---so that a single yardstick is applied
to both.

This requirement is not our invention. It is stated in the 2011 paper, whose
authors write that they ``cannot simply measure the conductance using the very
same sparsified graph, since that would tell us nothing about how well the
sparsified graph retained the cluster structure in the original graph,'' and who
report every quality figure on the original graph accordingly. In much of what
followed, and in the released evaluation code of at least one widely used
sparsification benchmark, the requirement is absent.

Its absence is not a minor bias. Across 63 controlled configurations spanning four
detection algorithms, two sparsifier families and seven networks, scoring on the
sparsified graph rather than the original reverses the sign of the conclusion in
45 of them. A pipeline that is harmful by 0.40 modularity under the correct
measurement can appear beneficial by 0.39 under the incorrect one. A literature
that measures this way cannot locate a boundary, because on the wrong side of the
yardstick everything succeeds.

% ---- B5: what we did -------------------------------------------------
We therefore rebuilt the evaluation before re-asking the question. Partitions are
always scored on the original graph. Comparisons against a stochastic baseline are
given the same wall-clock budget as the pipeline they are compared with, and are
also checked against a fixed-restart baseline, because a runtime-matched control
is weak precisely when the pipeline is fast. Because sparsification fragments
partitions, and because best-match and information-theoretic recovery scores rise
mechanically with the number of clusters, every recovery comparison is matched for
granularity against a partition of the original graph at the same cluster count
and is referenced to a chance floor computed from size-matched random partitions.
Because a heavy-tailed degree sequence alone can produce several of the effects
that are attributed to community structure, the central mechanistic claims are
checked against degree-preserving randomizations of the same graphs.

With that instrument we ran 23 controlled experiments over both sparsifier
families that have ever claimed quality gains---degree-based and
similarity-based, the latter being the 2011 method itself---four detection
algorithms drawn from two families, seventeen real networks of up to 25 million
edges, and a synthetic sweep of the one variable the original authors identified
as decisive. The design is intended to locate a boundary rather than to confirm or
refute a claim, and it is reported as such: predictions and stopping rules were
fixed before each experiment ran, and several of our own hypotheses did not
survive them.

% ---- B6: the boundary ------------------------------------------------
The boundary exists, and it is sharp.

Below it, sparsification does not help. For modularity-family detectors with free
granularity, on the sparse graphs that dominate practice---average degrees between
5 and 33---no sparsifier we tested improves modularity measured on the original
graph beyond the variation of a few random restarts, at any retention level that
preserves quality, under any of four algorithms. Nor is there a speed argument:
end-to-end, including the sparsifier's own cost, the pipeline runs at between
$0.87\times$ and $1.35\times$ the time of clustering the original graph directly,
and at aggressive retention it can be slower, because a fragmented graph gives the
optimizer more work rather than less. Every apparent gain we could find in this
regime---in modularity, in ground-truth recovery, and in the one case where a
sparsifier's edge-selection signal is demonstrably structure-aware rather than
degree-driven---dissolves when granularity is matched, when chance is subtracted,
or when the baseline is given an equal compute budget.

Above it, sparsification helps, and it helps for the reasons the 2011 authors
gave. On synthetic graphs with planted communities, holding the number of
communities fixed by construction so that no granularity effect is possible,
similarity-based sparsification raises both the objective measured on the original
graph and chance-corrected agreement with the planted labels once the average
degree passes approximately 50. The effect is absent at average degree 11 and 25,
present in two-thirds of configurations at 49, and present in all of them at 102
and 221; it survives the worst case over ten independent runs of the partitioner;
and it is not reproduced by degree-based or uniform sparsification at identical
retention, so it is a property of the similarity signal rather than of the edge
budget. Injecting spurious edges makes the gain grow monotonically, which is the
signature the denoising explanation predicts.

What decides the outcome, then, is not which sparsifier is used. It is the average
degree of the graph, the family of the detection algorithm---fixed-$k$ balanced
partitioners gain where free-granularity modularity optimizers do not---and
whether quality is measured against an external reference or against an objective
defined on the graph whose edges were deleted. The mechanism is consistent with
this: the quantity that governs a degree-based sparsifier's effect on modularity
is the separation between the scores it assigns to within-community and
between-community edges, and we show by direct intervention that this quantity is
controlled by where high-degree-product edges sit relative to community
boundaries, at fixed degree sequence, fixed partition and fixed modularity.

Two facts must travel with the positive result. Sparsification repairs a weaker
detector; it does not overtake a stronger one, and a resolution-tuned modularity
optimizer on the untouched graph outperforms every sparsified pipeline we
measured. And with an exact similarity computation the quality gain is not a speed
gain, because the sparsifier costs more than the detection it accelerates.

% ---- B7: contributions ----------------------------------------------
This paper contributes the following.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The boundary.} A degree threshold near average degree 50 and an
  algorithm-family split, established under controls that exclude granularity by
  construction, together with the negative territory delimited across two
  sparsifier families, four detection algorithms and seventeen networks.

  \item \textbf{An evaluation protocol, and the measured cost of omitting it.}
  Each control is motivated by a specific failure it catches, and the consequence
  of the central omission is quantified: 45 sign reversals in 63 configurations.
  We also introduce a permuted-weight control for similarity-weighted pipelines,
  which shows that the benefit attributed to similarity weighting is reproduced,
  and in one case exceeded, when the weights are randomly permuted across edges.

  \item \textbf{A mechanism for the boundary.} A decomposition of the
  score-separation quantity into a sorting term and a granularity term across
  seventeen networks, and a direct causal test in which that quantity is steered
  through more than a full sign change while degree sequence, partition,
  intra-edge fraction and modularity are all held exactly fixed.

  \item \textbf{An open phenomenon.} In several controlled settings the partitions
  that best recover reference communities are not the partitions that score best on
  the objective, and the two criteria move in opposite directions within the same
  configuration. We report this as an observation rather than a result, and argue
  that it is the most consequential open question the boundary exposes.
\end{itemize}
